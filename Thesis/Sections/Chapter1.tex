\par The Information Age has been one of the most challenging and revealing, yet fruitful times in the history of the world. For several decades in the early- to mid-twentieth century, a computer was considered a calculating machine, which consumed one or more rooms. The rooms, full of cabinets which contained blinking lights, controls, and the logic, arithmetic, and memory capabilities that makes computing possible, was seen as a wonder by many. Within the remainder of the twentieth century, the computer became known as a small device that could sit on someone's desk, in someone's lap, or within a cabinet in a data center. Within the first two decades of the twenty first century, though, the computer became much more than that. We now see them in our hands, on our heads, on our wrists, and beyond. Interestingly, over this nearly one hundred year period of expansion of the digital frontier, the computer is still fundamentally the same as it was in the beginning. The main difference now is the computer is magnitudes more performant and compact.

\par With changes in the size and scale of the computer comes changes in the means in which people interface with the computer. \gls{hci} - a field dedicated to the research, development, and innovation of how people use computing devices - has been a great influence in how the form factor and presentation of the computer has changed over time.\cite{Web-IDFHCI} Early \gls{hci} implementations include computer wiring patterns (e.g. \glsxtrshort{eniac}), punch cards (e.g. \glsxtrshort{univac}, \glsxtrshort{ibm} 1401), and bit manipulation by means of physical switches (e.g. Altair 8800, \glsxtrshort{imsai} 8080). In time, the keyboard was introduced, mimicking the familiar typewriters of the day.

\par However, in the late 1960s, the modern computer interface would be recognized for the first time - the mouse. Douglas Engelbart introduced radically new technology that floored the computer science community in what writer Steven Levy called ``The Mother of All Demos''. \cite{Book-InsanelyGreat} Unfortunately, it wouldn't be until years later when the keyboard and mouse (and its modern variants) would finally become the standard in interface for the computer.

\par Given the advances with \gls{hci} over time, we began seeing more interesting and experimental means of computer interface. \gls{xr} technology - a blend of prominent new methodologies for interacting with a virtual or enhanced world - is one such means. Within \gls{xr}, we see \gls{vr}, \gls{ar}, and \gls{mr}. \gls{vr} gives us the ability to create amazing, and sometimes even surreal or outright physically impossible, experiences contained within the bounds of hardware and software. \gls{ar} provides a context where virtual elements are imposed into the physical world. \gls{mr} offers the hybrid solution - virtual elements (seen either by means of \gls{vr} or \gls{ar}), being blended seamlessly into physical space. \cite{Web-HPXR}

\par While \gls{xr} is seen by the general public as a means of providing entertainment value (i.e. games, film, artistic experiences, etc.), it can also be used as a means of enhancing and expanding our understanding of the world around us. Some other examples of \gls{xr} in the world are the presentation of news and weather (i.e. title cards for news stories next to a news anchor, projections of weather conditions symbols onto maps) and online shopping experiences (i.e. websites where you can test fit eyeglasses \cite{Web-GlassesUSATryOnline}, clothing, etc.).

\par Although, one application of \gls{xr} seems to elude the public - \gls{hud} in automobiles. \gls{hud} technology has existed since the mid 20th century, but has been limited in application, namely for use in aircraft.

\par \gls{hud} solutions exist for automobiles today, which are either embedded in the dashboard of a vehicle by the manufacturer (either directly in front of or to the side of the driver), or are added by the driver through a third-party device which sits atop the instrument cluster. Each existing solution shares the same problem - limited dynamic information in a static format. Why must this be so? Must \gls{hud}s be restricted in presentation and configuration? With the emergence of new AR technology in the form of wearable glasses, a new solution can finally be explored to provide a more dynamic, and more fluid \gls{hud} experience to drivers.

\section{Problem Statement}
\par This thesis will explore an application of \gls{ar} for automobiles in the form of a dynamic \gls{hud}. By the end of this thesis, an \gls{ar}-based, software system spanning multiple devices will be designed and implemented that satisfies the application's needs.

\section{Inverse Requirements}
\par The following are the inverse requirements of this project. These anti-requirements define a boundary of work for the research and development of the system:
- No solution will be explored or developed to detect obstacles outside of embedded systems integrated in the test vehicle (e.g. \gls{cv} for object recognition).
- No solution will be explored or developed to block other applications from running on the compute device (e.g. \gls{os} functions).

The inverse requirements of the project, as well as the full set of project requirements, are described in chapter 3.
