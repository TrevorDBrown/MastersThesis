\section{Summary}
    \par DashAR (pronounced either as it looks: a portmanteau of ``Dashboard'' and ``\gls{ar}'', or like Dasher the Reindeer) is a dynamic \gls{hud} solution for drivers of automobiles. It provides drivers near real-time information, such as automobile's speed in space, the engine's rotational speed internally, the amount of fuel remaining in the fuel tank, and so on. These are pieces of information generally found on the instrument cluster of most automobiles.

\section{Requirements}
    \par During the development of IBM's Watson platform, a massively parallel computation project in the early 2010s, Dr. Bijan Davari, \gls{ibm} Technical Fellow and Vice President of Next Generation Computing Systems and Technology, stated, regarding application development at the scale of Watson, that ``[The] application is the king. Start with that, what are the key requirements for that, and how do they need to be satisfied. You bring the elements that are required [together] to take care of that application need.'' \cite{IBM2012} In other words, design the software first, then proceed to bring in the necessary resources as they are needed. That is the approach taken with this project.

    \subsection{Functional Requirements}
        \par In order to determine what kind of system needs to be designed, a set of functional requirements must be defined to establish base expectations. These requirements drive the research and development of the system, as they describe what the system will do. For the DashAR System, the following functional requirements are defined:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item The DashAR System shall provide a \gls{hud} within an \gls{ar} environment, presenting a real-time display of information while operating an automobile.
            \item The DashAR System shall provide the \gls{hud} information dynamically, in form and in content, as defined by the user via configuration files.
            \item The DashAR System shall retrieve data from the automobile via the \gls{obdii} interface.
            \item The DashAR System shall store data, either collected by or generated by the system, into a local database instance, for analytics and historical context.
        \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Extended Requirements}
        \par While determining functional requirements, additional requirements arose that, while seemingly ideal, may be difficult to complete in the alloted timeframe. For the DashAR System, the following extended requirements were realized and defined:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item The DashAR System shall provide extensibility via an Extensions framework, utilizing configuration files and custom scripts to extend system functionality.
            \item The DashAR System shall provide support for third-party \glspl{api} via the Extensions framework, providing data and insights to enhance the driver's experience while operating the automobile.
            \item The DashAR System shall provide facilities for end users to easily customize \gls{hud} layouts, such as a web-based configuration application.
            \item The DashAR System shall provide additional operational information via Extensions, including \gls{ecu} error code information, turn signal indication, and blind spot detection.
        \end{itemize}

        \par These extended requirements, like functional requirements, provide an expectation of the system's potential capabilities. However, if time constraints prevail, these requirements can evolve into inverse requirements, in the sense that they could be overlooked during the development process. These requirements may sometimes be referred to as ``stretch goals''.

    \subsection{Non-Functional Requirements}
        \par Some requirements defined are more subjective in nature, relying on qualitative data not expressible in traditional metrics. For the DashAR system, the following non-functional requirements are defined:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item The DashAR System will utilize integrations between the various hardware subsystems, originating from the automobile, to any intermediate devices used for computation, to the \gls{ar} display.
            \item The DashAR System will utilize lightweight, wearable electronic eyeglasses to satisfy the \gls{ar} needs.
            \item The DashAR System will provide a performant experience while in operation.
            \item The DashAR System will handle multiple streams of input simultaneously and in real-time.
        \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Inverse Requirements}
        \par The following inverse requirements are defined for this project. These ``anti-requirements'' define a boundary of work for the research and development of this system, where there will be no focus or energy expelled:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item The DashAR System shall not implement functionality to block other applications from running on any device within in the system, such as platform-compatible entertainment and social applications and additional \gls{os} services.
            \item The DashAR System shall not natively incorporate \gls{ml} and \gls{ai} functionality.
            \item The DashAR System shall not natively incorporate \gls{cv} support for object recognition, even if functionally enabling components were available, such as cameras, sensor arrays, and \gls{ml} models.
        \end{itemize}

        \par The primary dilemma with addressing these requirements in this research is having the available time. Therefore, these inverse requirements are good candidates for future work, either by this author or future researchers seeking to expand on this topic.

\section{DashAR's Design}
    \par After determining the various requirements of the DashAR System, a basic framework was designed that would handle the following basic functions:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item An \gls{ar} component, for rendering an automotive \gls{hud}.
        \item A computer, for capturing data from various feeds, like \gls{obdii} data and the internet.
        \item Middleware software, to run on the computer, that would aggregate and serve the data to the automotive \gls{hud}.
        \item A basic wireless network, for communication between devices.
        \item Interchangeable, system-agnostic components.
    \end{itemize}

    \par This framework led to the realization of the components which comprise the DashAR System: the automobile, the \gls{ics}, and the \gls{ar} subsystem. This framework is visualized in Figure \ref{fig:dashartechstack}.

    \subsection{System Components}
        \subsubsection{Automobile}
            \par The automobile, being the core component of DashAR, is any gasoline powered automobile manufactured after 1996, or any diesel powered automobile after 1997. This is because the automobile manufacturers are required to have \gls{obdii} \cite{CEPA2019}. Considering that the mean age of an automobile is 12 years in the \glsxtrshort{usa} \cite{BTS2024} and 18 years in Western Europe \cite{Held2021}, it is safe to assume a wide array of people will be able to use DashAR as designed, with little to no modification. Unfortunately, even with the rapid growth in market share around \glspl{ev} in society today, these automobiles are not guaranteed to have \gls{obdii} available, thus breaking functionality with the DashAR system. Since \gls{obdii} interfaces directly with the \gls{ecu} of the automobile, \gls{can} communications are internally well-established, provide real-time data. The primary consideration when dealing with this data is finding an effective way to extract it.

        \subsubsection{Intermediate Computing Subsystem}
            \par The \gls{ics} is the data aggregation and brokering platform of the DashAR system. The \gls{ics} is what allows the \gls{ar} subsystem to communicate with the automobile, as well as other data sources. Based on the research of \cite{Doshi2009}, it was determined a \gls{pc} would be needed that met the following criteria:

            \begin{itemize}
                \item Physically Portable: the computer should be able to fit into the automobile easily.
                \item Energy Consumption: the computer should be able to be powered by the automobile while driving, or by an external power source such as a \gls{ups} or battery bank.
                \item Computing Power: the computer should be able to process data from the automobile and other inputs in near real-time (i.e. within, at most, 500 ms/s).
                \item Unattended Operation: the computer should be able to run without direct interaction from the user, with the exception of the system shutdown process.
                \item Connectivity: the computer should be able to interface with \gls{usb} devices, Bluetooth devices, and Wi-Fi networks.
                \item Easily Programmable: the computer should be able to run both system software and bespoke application software.
            \end{itemize}

            \par Fortunately, there exists a form factor of \gls{pc}, which is readily available on the market, that meet the criteria specified: the \gls{sbc}. \Glspl{sbc} are \glspl{pc} that are approximately the size of a credit card. They are generally powered by \glsxtrshort{arm}-based chips, having a wide range of performance capabilities. An example of one can be found in Figure \ref{fig:rpi5}.

            \par Once an ideal computing platform was determined, the methodology in which the data from the automobile could be extracted had to be determined as well. There exists a standard interface between \gls{obdii} and serial, called ELM327. The ELM327 can signal the \gls{ecu} to provide specific details regarding the automobile's operational state. In turn, the ELM327 can package the responses from the \gls{ecu} into a format usable by an \gls{obdii} scanning tool or \gls{pc} running \gls{obdii} interpreter software.

            \par ELM327-based adapters exist several serial interface formats, from wired (e.g. \gls{obdii}-to-\gls{usb} and \gls{obdii}-to-RS232 (Standard Serial)) to wireless (e.g. \gls{obdii}-to-Bluetooth and \gls{obdii}-to-Wi-Fi). For the purposes of this project, the DashAR system was designed with a wired connection in mind, due to its consistent reliability. Considering the ubiquitous nature of \gls{usb}, it was the logical choice in wired options.

            \par With connectivity established between the automobile and the \gls{ics}, the final component is ready to be integrated.

        \subsubsection{AR Subsystem}
            \par The \gls{ar} Subsystem is the platform in which the \gls{hud} is displayed. When the project began, Meta's Quest 3 \gls{ar} headset was the most commonplace \gls{ar} product that fit the application needs. However, due to the bulkiness of the headset, and the legal concerns regarding \gls{vr} utilization while operating an automobile, this product selection was quickly abandoned.

            \par While searching for alternative options, a relatively unknown product came to the forefront, which, on paper, seemed like an ideal resolution for these concerns: \gls{ar} glasses, namely the Air 2 line from XREAL (formerly NREAL). The Air 2 Pro \gls{ar} glasses is a small form factor \gls{ar} solution that is approximately the size of a pair of large sunglasses. Since their founding in 2017, XREAL has released multiple models of their \gls{ar} glasses, as well as companion devices: the XREAL Beam and XREAL Beam Pro \cite{XREAL2025}.

            \par At the start of development, the Air 2 Pro \gls{ar} glasses were considered to be XREAL's mid-tier offering, as they either lacked certain functionality found in the higher end model or were limited in their function (e.g. front facing cameras, 6-\gls{dof} as opposed to the Air 2 Pro's 3-\gls{dof}, etc.) However, all \gls{ar} glasses from XREAL use Sony's \gls{oled} microdisplay technology \cite{SSSC2025}, which makes the \gls{ar} experience possible.

    \subsection{Software Components}
            \subsubsection{DAS API}
                \par The modern automobile captures many data points, events, and system states via the \gls{ecu} several times a second. This data is accessible over the \gls{obdii} interface, generally for the purposes of diagnostics. However, there is a potential for this data to be captured in real time, while the automobile is in operation.

                \par The \gls{das} is the software subsystem responsible for the collection, aggregation, and distribution of data, from varying sources within the automobile to the visualization of the data via the \gls{hud}. The \gls{das} is hosted on the \gls{ics}. The \gls{ecu} is one of many potential data sources while operating the automobile. Data can also be collected from additional devices and sensors on board, such as those found in mobile phones, computers, and other electronics. With a cellular modem, even the internet is accessible as a data source, expanding the potential for services that the DashAR System can provide.

                \par Given this wide array of data sources, it's important to have a software subsystem capable of managing these resources effectively, efficiently, and dynamically. To satisfy these conditions, the \gls{das} was designed to function as a client-server, \gls{tcpip}-oriented \gls{api}. Exposed endpoints allow for data to be retrieved from the various sources via GET requests from an \gls{http} client.

            \subsubsection{HUD}
                \par To satisfy the setup of the \gls{das} \gls{api}, the \gls{hud} software is designed with a basic \gls{http} client acting as the central context for the \gls{ar} subsystem's interaction with the \gls{ics}. When data is needed, the \gls{hud} would send requests to the appropriate endpoints of the \gls{api}. In turn, the \gls{api} would provide a response, formatted in a standard data serialization format acceptable by the \gls{hud}. The responses would be tailored to the requested endpoint (i.e. the /data/obdii endpoint would result in \gls{obdii} data, the /data/map would results in map data, etc.)

                \par Based on the user's needs, the \gls{hud} interface layout would be customizable, but with a couple of caveats. The first caveat: a limited palette of \gls{ui} components. These components are:

                \begin{itemize}
                    \item Widgets: also known as a gauge, these are the basic components that allow data from the automobile and other data sources to be displayed.
                    \item Trays: the base \gls{ui} element in which widgets are affixed. By default, two trays are used: top tray and bottom tray.
                    \item Tray Anchors: the specific, equidistant points on a given tray in which a widget can affixed. By default, only 10 tray anchors are used, 5 per tray, addressible by specifying a tray prefix and relative position, such as T1-T5 or B1-B5.
                \end{itemize}

                \par Widgets are assigned a data source, down to a specific data point (e.g. speedometer -> speed data, tachometer -> RPM data, etc.) From there, as data is retrieved from the \gls{das} \gls{api}, the widgets are automatically updated with the provided data.

\section{An Ideal Scenario}
    \par When thinking through the design of the DashAR System, an ideal scenario is utilized to set a baseline for what the user's experience should be. Ideally, the following experience would occur for the user of the system:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The user configures the \gls{ics} device before entering the automobile. This would be achieved by the user powering on their \gls{ics} device via a standard power outlet, based on their country's electrical standards. Then, the user would connect to the device directly via Wi-Fi or ethernet. Finally, the user would load a configuration app hosted on the device. Once the desired configuration is achieved, the user powers down the \gls{ics} device, either by pressing a power button, or in software.
        \item The user connects the ELM327 adapter into the \gls{obdii} port on their automobile. The user connects the other end of the adapter into a USB port on their \gls{ics} device.
        \item The user powers on their \gls{ics} device, using either an automobile-integrated or external power inverter.
        \item After 30 seconds, the user connects their \gls{ar} subsystem compute device to the Wi-Fi emitted by the \gls{ics} device.
        \item Once connectivity is established between the \gls{ics} device and the \gls{ar} subsystem compute device, the user connects their \gls{ar} glasses to the associated compute device, and opens the \gls{hud} application.
        \item Once opened, the \gls{hud} application will initialize, by sending a request to the \gls{ics} device for the \gls{hud}'s base configuration and the custom interface configuration. The configuration is interpreted and rendered by the \gls{hud} application.
        \item Once the \gls{hud} is rendered, the \gls{hud} application will poll the \gls{ics} device for data on a set frequency. As responses are received, the \gls{hud}'s widgets automatically update with near real-time data.
        \item The user drives the automobile.
        \item Once the drive has concluded, the user will close the \gls{hud} application. The user will also power down the \gls{ics} device, by pressing the power button. All remaining activity on the \gls{ics} device will cease, and result in a safe shutdown.
        \item If desired, the user can retrieve the data collected during the automobile's operation from the \gls{ics} device at a later time.
    \end{enumerate}

    \par Obviously, this scenario does not take into account error conditions (e.g. disconnects between subsystems, \gls{hud} application failure, etc.) or delays (e.g. responses greater than one second from the \gls{ics} when making a request, \gls{hud} application slowdown resulting in data being rendered late, etc.) However, it does provide a primary path of success for the system, in which errors can be addressed throughout via branching logic and system/data validations.

\section{Why DashAR?}
    \par Considering the design of the DashAR system, a question that may come to someone's mind when thinking about this approach: ``Why bother with a solution like DashAR, and just look down at the instrument cluster while I drive?''. Admittedly, for many people, that is the most practical solution. After all, the instrument cluster is readily available, generally easy to follow, and is there to help the driver.

    \par However, the biggest problem with the instrument cluster today is that, in most automobiles, the information presented is fixed. The instrument cluster cannot easily be adjusted to meet the needs of every individual that operates a specific automobile. The information presented within an instrument cluster is also at the mercy of the manufacturer. With the DashAR System, the user has control over the information presented to them as they drive.
