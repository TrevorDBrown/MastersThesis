\section{Automotive Instrumentation and Heads Down Displays}
    \par Automotive Instrumentation is the representation of critical operation data easily and quickly consumable by the operator of the automobile. When instrumentation in an automobile is mentioned in research and amongst the general populace, it is referring to the instrument cluster, which sits directly in front of driver. It is generally embedded within the dashboard. Some of the most commonly presented instruments, or gauges, within an instrument cluster include:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Speedometer, which presents the automobile's absolute velocity.
        \item Tachometer, which presents the automobile's crankshaft speed, in \glspl{rpm}.
        \item Fuel Gauge, which presents the current fuel level relative to the automobile's fuel tank's capacity.
        \item Temperature Gauges, which presents the temperature of core systems (e.g. the engine (oil) and the radiator (water)).
        \item Odometer, which presents the miles driven in the automobile since manufacture.
        \item Information and Warning Lamps, which illuminate based on certain conditions (e.g. \gls{abs} problem, headlights are on, no seatbelt, low tire pressure, engine problems, etc.)
    \end{itemize}

    \par Given the commonplace nature of instrumentation in automobiles today, it is hard to imagine a time where that was not the case. In 1886, Carl Benz introduced what is considered the first modern automobile: his Patent-Motorwagen, or ``patented motorcar''. This automobile offered very limited space to the occupants. In turn, no instrumentation could be provided \cite{Knoll2017}. It was not until sixteen years later, in 1902, when Otto Schulze, a German engineer, introduced the first instrument implemented in an automobile: a speedometer \cite{Knoll2017}.

    \par Since then, evolutionary efforts were made to the idea of providing instrumentation to the automobile operator. The 1914 Ford Model T offered a speedometer and an ammeter. These early automotive gauges were fully mechanical, interfacing directly with the various components they monitored \cite{Knoll2017}. Eventually, these gauges became digital, evolving into a system where the \gls{ecu} of the automobile monitors the various subsystems, and reports the data to their respective gauges and displays \cite{Knoll2017}. The exposure of the \gls{ecu} to external devices came to fruition in 1996, with the introduction of the \gls{obdii} protocol. Originally introduced, to satisfy \glsxtrshort{usa} federal government regulations \cite{EPA1996}, as well as regulations at the state level, specifically the State of California \cite{CEPA2019}, additional uses were identified, as this protocol could provide operating data and other useful information.

    \par For many automobile owners, their only interactions with \gls{obdii} have been limited to automobile problems. When they see the ``Check Engine'' light illuminate in their instrument cluster, they take their automobile to a repair garage or auto parts store. From there, a mechanic or technician connects a specialized computer, or ``scanner'', to the \gls{obdii} port, that allows them to identify and receive the error codes behind the warning lamp. However, there is so much more potential for \gls{obdii}.

    \par Research by \cite{Godavarty2000} demonstrates an approach for interfacing a generic \gls{pc} to an automobile via \gls{obdii}. In turn, the \gls{pc}, running custom software, can interpret the data being received over \gls{obdii}, allowing it to be used for many types of applications, such as historical reporting on a specific automobile's operation.

    \par The format in which automotive instrumentation is commonly displayed is considered a \gls{hdd}. These \gls{hdd} work for a variety of applications, by presenting information to a subject, at the cost of temporarily taking their focus away from their operating environment (e.g. the roadway while driving, the skies while flying, the sea while boating, etc.) For some situations, this is acceptable. However, there are inherent risks when operating heavy, even dangerous, machinery under adverse conditions \cite{Smith2016}. Given this, what alternatives exists to such an arrangement?

\section{Heads Up Displays}
    \par The origins of \glspl{hud} can be traced back to the beginning of the 20th century. In 1900, an Irish Optical Engineer, Howard Grubb, devised a new kind of gun sight which used a mirror to superimpose a reticle into a visible field \cite{Grubb1902}. The gun operator would use this sight, known as a reflector (or reflex) sight, to aim at a target, and shoot it. As time progressed, the reflector sight was enlarged, and became more integral in military applications. During \gls{wwii}, the Allied Ground Combat and Naval forces utilizes reflector sights, to target enemies more accurately \cite{Suits1948}.

    \par Over the remainder of the 20th century, many other advancements and innovations in science and engineering behind \gls{hud} technology were made. \cite{Nobles1958} introduced a method for projecting information onto the windshield of an aircraft, namely radar information within their macular and near peripheral vision. This would allow pilots to have both their target in view, as well as more detailed information about the relative position of that targeted aircraft.

    \par In the early 1970s, developments by a team of engineers at General Electric led to the creation of a \gls{hud} that was rendered from a \gls{crt} display onto a piece of glass affixed to the aircraft windshield. This arrangement allowed for highly dynamic content to be display, such as instrumentation of the aircraft, without blocking the pilot's field of view \cite{Antonson1973}.

    \par \cite{Withrington1976}, with the Hughes Aircraft Company, introduced an approach that involved the use of holographic lenses to achieve a similar effect. However, instead of projection onto the windshield of the aircraft, the projection would be made within the pilot's helmet. While independent of the developments in \gls{ar}, this can be seen as a precursor to the technology.

    \par By the 1980s, \glspl{hud} were in use in commercial aviation. By 1988, the first \gls{hud} for automobiles was introduced, specifically the 1988 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme. This achievement in implementing a smaller \gls{hud} system was done through research efforts by collaboration between the General Motors Corporation, Hughes Aircraft Company's Automotive Systems division, and Delco Electronics. It utilizes a \gls{vfd}, which reflected into the automobile's windshield \cite{Weihrauch1989,Jurgen1998}.

    \par Since the 1980s, automotive \gls{hud} research and development has continued at great lengths. \cite{Doshi2009} explored a \gls{hud} option that displays alerts to the driver through a ``Dynamic Active Display'', as shown in Figure \ref{fig:dadhud}. Specifically, the \gls{hud} alerted drivers if their automobile exceeded the speed limit, either numerically, symbolically, or through graphical charts. Something of note from this research, and how it is applied to the automotive \gls{hud} solution described later is its incorporation of a standard \gls{pc}, a 1000 W power inverter, and interfaces into the \gls{can} Bus.

    \par Further research by \cite{Smith2016} has explored the potential benefits of a \gls{hud} over the traditional \gls{hdd} found in automobiles. Using a driving simulator equipped with an aftermarket \gls{hud} device, they were able to measure subject's ability to perform secondary tasks while simultaneously driving. Consistent parameters were set to ensure consistently across all participants. The primary goal: follow a white lead automobile while traveling at a fixed speed. The subject, while attempting to achieve the primary goal, was tasked with observing a randomized string of text rendered, either rendered into the \gls{hud} or the \gls{hdd}, locating a sequential, repetitive character. Then, a new randomized string was presented. The subject would then attempt to identify the frequency of the previously identified character \cite{Smith2016}.

    \par The purpose of this secondary task was to act as a distraction from the environment. This was verified by a pair of specialized glasses that were worn by the subject that tracked eye movement. After the results of these tests were examined through the lens of the \gls{nhtsa} guidelines on distractions while driving \cite{NHTSA2013}, a conclusion was reached. The research found that while the data retrieved via the \gls{hud} was processed quicker than the \gls{hdd}, with similar accuracy, the \gls{nhtsa} guidelines were not satisfied, meaning the \gls{hud} would be unfit for road worthiness. \cite{Smith2016} suggested that these guidelines should be reviewed ``with \glspl{hud} in mind'', and potentially be ``governed by a different set of guidelines''.

\section{Extended Reality, Computer Graphics, and Human-Computer Interaction}
    \par Ivan Sutherland, an early pioneer in the fields of \gls{cg} and \gls{vr}, once said, ``The screen is a window through which one sees a virtual world. The challenge is to make that world look real, act real, sound real, feel real.'' \cite{Garassini1994} In this context, the ``screen'' Sutherland is referring to is the literal screen of a computer: the display. In Sutherland's early days (1960s), a computer display would have been a \gls{crt}. When paired with a keyboard, it would become an interface into a remote time-shared computer.

    \par Before Sutherland's landmark doctoral dissertation: ``Sketchpad, A Man-Machine Graphical Communication System'' \cite{Sutherland1963}, the field of computer graphics was considered still in its infancy. In his thesis, shown in Figure \ref{fig:sketchpaddemo}, Sutherland introduced new, interesting concepts related to the way people interacted with machines. The graphics used in Sketchpad were very primitive: basic, un-shaded 2D polygons. The interface for the Sketchpad system was also revolutionary, yet very primitive by today's standards. A light pen: a device that uses sensors to detect light on a screen, along with a series of manually controlled limit switches, and a monitor, were used to create, display, and manipulate Sketchpad's graphics. Yet, even with all of the primitive aspects of the system, many of today's commonplace software development techniques and attributes were introduced, either explicitly or implicitly: interactive computer graphics, object-oriented programming, and non-procedural programming languages \cite{Kay2017}. Even through all of the progress that was made, it would be some time before \gls{cg} would become anything that would be considered close to realism.

    \par A little over a decade after Sketchpad, a Ph.D candidate at the University of Utah explored new and efficient means of producing curved surfaces. The student, Edwin Catmull, advised by Sutherland himself, produced what is considered another novel research into \gls{cg}: ``A Subdivision Algorithm for Computer Display of Curved Surfaces'' \cite{Catmull1974}. He, along with other University of Utah students, later co-founded an animation company known as Pixar Animation Studios, known for their award-winning animated films and advancements in computer animation technology (e.g. RenderMan \cite{Raghavachary2006}). Many now standard computer graphics technologies and techniques either came from or were enhanced by Pixar: ray tracing (the projection of light in virtual environments) \cite{Cook1984}, model texturing enhancements \cite{Peachey1990}, and others.

    \par Other efforts, while not \gls{cg}-centric, utilized \gls{cg} in innovative and expressive ways. In the early 1970s, Terry Winograd, Computer Science Professor Emeritus at Stanford University and renowned expert in \gls{hci}, developed a \gls{cg}-based \gls{ai} program called SHRDLU \cite{Winograd1971,Winograd1999}. SHRDLU was an innovative \gls{nlp} system. However, it was limited in its domain. The system could only manipulate a micro-world containing basic elements: blocks, pyramids, and a box. Each element was assigned a color: red, green, or blue. The user could prompt the system to manipulate the elements (e.g. ``PICK UP THE RED PYRAMID, AND PLACE IT IN THE BOX''). The prompts could be used to target a group of elements based on a shared property (e.g. all red objects, all pyramids), or specific element (e.g. the red pyramid, the green block).

    \par The earliest implementation of SHRDLU lacked any shading for the graphics, which made it difficult to track elements. However, later efforts by students at the University of Utah introduced color rendering, thus improving the comprehension of the environment \cite{Winograd1971}. As seen in Figure \ref{fig:shrdlujava}, there have been other implementations of SHRDLU beyond the original. In retrospect, this micro-world could be considered a primitive representation of the physical world around us; rather, a form of world simulation.

    \par From there, \gls{cg} evolved by leaps and bounds. \gls{vr} technology was impacted by these advancements as well, as the rendered graphics within the \gls{vr} environments became more defined and clearer. The 1990s saw the introduction of consumer-grade \gls{vr} headsets, such as the Sony Glasstron \cite{Rekimoto1998}. Even with these advancements, these types of \gls{vr} headsets were still prohibitively expensive for the general public.

    \par Then, in 2011, an 18 year old technology enthusiast named Palmer Luckey developed a \gls{vr} prototype of what would become the Oculus Rift \cite{Mortensen2015}. The revolutionary change that the Oculus Rift \gls{vr} headset brought the world was akin to that of Henry Ford's introduction of the assembly line for automobile manufacturing. \gls{vr} became widely available to the general populace. Since then, \gls{xr} technology in general has seen significant investments in research and development.

    \par In all of these efforts which shaped the fields of \gls{xr}, \gls{cg}, \gls{hci}, and implicitly, the field of computing in general, one must recognize the organized, collaborative efforts of the software engineers and artists behind the screen. Without software engineering, these feats would not have been possible.

\section{Software Engineering and the Agile Model}
    \par While \gls{xr} and \gls{cg} have had revolutionary and evolutionary changes over the course of time, software engineering has followed a similar pattern. The term ``software engineering'' was coined by Margaret Hamilton: one of NASA's programmers during the Apollo missions to the moon \cite{Booch2018}. While her contemporaries were against the phrase, perceiving software engineering as a ``fad'', the field has emerged in a rapid and very expansive fashion. What solidified this new field of engineering, and prevented it from falling into obscurity, was its structured approach to design, implementation, and testing. Other engineering disciplines are known for such practices.

    \par One of the greatest tools at the disposal of a software engineer is a development model: a structure process flow in which software is developed. One of the earliest software engineering models was the Waterfall development model. Waterfall is comprised of sequential, interdependent processes \cite{Sommerville2011}.

    \par Waterfall begin with requirements gathering \cite{Sommerville2011}. In this phase, the software engineer determines the problem and needs of the users. In private development, a software engineer would interview the end users for the details of what the software system should and should not do. For larger, public-oriented developments, market research and feedback on a larger scale is necessary. During this time, a feasibility study may be performed. This will allow a software engineer to determine if the proposed work is even technically feasible, given the state of computing technology at that point in time. Generally, and ideally, a software engineer may work within the confines of a defined software stack: a set of software which offers the resources necessary to make a user-level application. Consistent software stacks promote software that's easier to maintain and consistent across an organization \cite{Sommerville2011}.

    \par Once these requirements are gathered, the next phase of the Waterfall is the System Design \cite{Sommerville2011}. During this phase, many technical details are explored and defined. What technology required to develop a solution to the problem? Are data sources needed? How is the data stored and retrieved? How will the user interacts with the software? Many other questions and possibilities are analyzed and explored. During this phase, a series of documents may be produced which explain processes and procedures for system function and interaction. These documents, generally referred to as \gls{uml} diagrams \cite{Medvidovic2002}, define, in a clean, consistent fashion, system behavior, user expectations, and technical solutions. Once a majority of the pending questions are answered, and design documents are complete, a software design is realized. The implementation phase begins \cite{Sommerville2011}.

    \par Implementation is the process of developing a software solution based on the generated design \cite{Sommerville2011}. If the software design is of high quality (i.e. concise details, defined use cases, etc.), a software engineer should be able to implement a solution relatively fast. If the design is low quality (i.e. lacking detail, holes in information, technically infeasible, etc.), a lot of unnecessary time can be spent implementing a solution that does not meet the requirements initially established. It is important for quality to be preserved throughout the entire process, so the most effective solution can be implemented as efficiently as possible \cite{Sommerville2011}. During implementation, the software engineer adheres to their organization's practices and standards when programming, as well as those defined by their stack's respective standards (i.e. \gls{posix} if using a UNIX-based operating system, \gls{tcpip} if developing a web application, etc.). Again, such guidelines and rules help promote the development of clean, consistent applications.

    \par Once the implementation is complete, the software is thoroughly tested \cite{Sommerville2011}. This process requires the software engineer to try the use cases defined in the Design phase, and verify they can be successfully achieved. Erroneous conditions are also tested (invalid inputs, out of order operations, etc.). This is to verify the software can handle and/or recover from the potential faults, or gracefully terminate. As software issues (colloquially known in the software engineering community as ``bugs'') are found, the software engineer proceeds to adjust the solution accordingly. Eventually, after refinement is complete, a hopefully stable product is created, and ready for release and longer term maintenance: the final phase of Waterfall \cite{Sommerville2011}.

    \par Once released, the end users begin using the solution. As time progresses, more bugs may be discovered, or new features may be requested. Depending on the structure of the software and context of the solution, the same software engineer who designed and implemented the solution may also perform the maintenance operations. In larger organizations and for public software, another software engineer may perform the maintenance, depending on the availability of the original source code. Some organizations rely on multiple people to achieve the same end result. Product/Program Managers, Software Analysts, and other design-oriented roles may perform the Requirements Gathering and System Design. Software Developers/Engineers may solely implement a solution. \gls{qa} engineers and testers verify the software's integrity and function. Each role, feeding into the next, achieved by a collective group.

    \par While Waterfall provided an effective, familiar means of implementing a software solution, it is not perfect. One of its issues is its general flow: sequential, very detail driven from the start, thus generally resulting in longer development times. While this process works for some solutions, other solutions require a faster means of software release and maintenance. A variant of Waterfall, named ``Modified Waterfall'', offers a means of backtracking to either gather more requirements, refine the design, or re-implement part or all of the solution. While this may help with some of the initial needs (i.e. missed requirements, oversights in design, etc.), it may extend a software solution's release even further, or may prevent a release at all.

    \par Some individuals saw the Waterfall model, and decided they could implement a better version of it. That model would become Agile Software Engineering \cite{Sommerville2011}. Agile attempts to resolve some of the issues of Waterfall. While it follows the same phases as Waterfall, the phases are handled a lot faster, in smaller chunks, and with respect to time constraints. Agile models are broken into fixed interval development cycles, or sprints. During a sprint, software engineers attempt to fully implement a defined solution. These solutions, again, are a lot smaller than those in Waterfall. For practical management of the sprint, a sprint board is used. The board consists of multiple columns, or swimlanes. Each swimlane is defined for specific states of the work (e.g. To Do, In Progress, Completed, Cancelled, etc.) Within each swimlane, work items, or cards, are defined. These cards are the overarching work to be done, that results in some kind of deliverable (e.g. new or modified features, refactoring and cleanup of code, documentation, etc.)

    \par Based on the results of the sprint, more sprints may be needed to extend, enhance, re-implement, or remove functionality. This results in software being released in a more rapid fashion. Ultimately, the end users are able to provide feedback throughout the process. This allows the software to be value-added, and reduces the chances of non-useful software being developed.

    \par Practical examples of Agile being used in society can be found in the heart of Silicon Valley. Companies such as Google \cite{Winters2020} and Facebook \cite{Feitelson2013} utilize a ``quick and often release'' mentality, enabled through the use of Agile development. While this sounds potentially reckless and unprofessional, this model of development has the potential to uncover and correct issues faster than traditional Waterfall-based models.

    \par The Agile methodology, in ways, mirrors the scientific method applied in research, and theses such as this one. A question is asked: Is a dynamic \gls{hud} for automobiles technically feasible? The hypothesis: a dynamic \gls{hud} for automobiles is possible. The experiment: implementing a solution. Finally, testing and evaluating the experiment: testing and using the solution. With that said, Agile methodology, when applied to research, is incredibly useful and practical.
