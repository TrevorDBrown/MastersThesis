\section{Methodology}
    \par The design and implementation of the DashAR system was handled through iterative development practices via Agile. Each iteration of the project consisted of two week sprints, in which work was identified, evaluated, performed, completed, deferred, or a combination of these. The development process formally began on Sunday, August 18th, 2024 with sprint 1, and ended on Saturday, March 29th, 2025 with sprint 16. There were brief interruptions in development for various purposes, from writing this thesis (which was tracked in sprint) to impediments that prevented progress (untracked).

    \begin{figure}[h!]
        \vspace{0.5cm}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=0.25]{./Support/Images/DashAR-SprintBoard.png}
        \caption{Sprint 14 Sprint Board in Obsidian}
        \label{fig:dasharsprintboard}
    \end{figure}

    \par Sprints were tracked using the Obsidian \gls{pkb} software \cite{Obsidian2025}. An example of this tracking can be seen in figure \ref{fig:dasharsprintboard}. A community module exists for managing Agile sprint boards. Each sprint consisted of a sprint board, with six swimlanes: To Do, In Progress, On Hold, Testing, Completed, and Rollover. Cards were initially added to the ``To Do'' swimlane, and progressed into the others.

    \par ``To Do'' was designated for cards that were waiting to be addressed. ``In Progress'' contained those cards actively being developed. ``On Hold'' contained cards that were unable to continue further into development, for any number of reasons (e.g. a roadblock prevent further work, waiting on a dependency to be worked on first, etc.) ``Testing'' was for cards that had been fully implemented, but needed verification of their functionality against the rest of the system. ``Completed'' contained the cards that were fully implemented and incorporated into the system. Finally, ``Rollover'' contained the cards that were to move into the next sprint.

    \par On Saturdays, cards not in the ``Completed'' swimlane were reviewed to verify their readiness. If a card was completed by the end of the day, it was moved into ``Completed''. If a card was not fully completed by the end of the day, it would be moved into ``Rollover'', to be worked on next sprint. If for any reason a card was no longer needed (e.g. a planned feature ultimately not included in the system, erroneous cards like duplications, etc.), it would be moved to ``Completed'', and the text ``(CANCELLED)'' would be prepended to the title of the card.

    \par Each card had a corresponding Markdown file, which contained more details regarding the work. The sections of the Markdown file were: Tasks and Updates. ``Tasks'' were the step-by-step items needed to complete the overarching work item. ``Updates'' were anecdotal notes, grouped by date, with information assisting with development. An entry was added to Updates for each new cards, signifying the date in which the card was introduced. This helped with tracking the amount of time a card took to get from ``To Do'' to ``Completed''.

    \par Some cards merited implementation that could not be achieved during the development of the system. The title of those cards were prepended with ``(FUTURE WORK)'', to signify their usefulness to the system. An example early on in development of this was the ``Determine uninterrupted power supply issue solution.'' This work item was designed to address a problem faced with the \gls{das} device immediately losing power as soon as the car was turned off. Since the \gls{das} device was a Raspberry Pi, some solutions existed involving batteries (i.e. \gls{ups}) that would help solve the problem. However, it was still not clear of the efficacy of those solutions, as they were bulky, and the batteries were exposed. After some time, a workaround was advised, suggesting that before the car is turned off, the power button on the \gls{das} device should be pressed. This would safely power down the device, stopping running services, such as the \gls{das} \gls{api}.

    \par With an Agile methodology defined, development of the DashAR System is now achievable in a structured, iterative way.

\section{The Implementation}
    \par The implementation of the DashAR System, following the Agile methodology prescribed, resulted in a usable \gls{ar}-based software system, which met the defined functional requirements. The following subsections detail the development process for each subsystem. Associated source code can be found in Appendix \ref{appendix:project}.

        \subsubsection{Hardware}
            \par Following the design specified in \Cref{chap:design}, the first implemented subsystem was the \gls{ics} and \gls{das} \gls{api}. The computing platform selected for the \gls{ics} was the \gls{rpi} 5, manufactured by \cite{RPL2025}. \gls{rpi} 5 is a \gls{sbc}, released in 2023, which meets all of the criteria defined for the \gls{ics}. The \gls{rpi} 5 uses an industry standard, ARM-based processor, operating at 2.4 GHz, with \gls{ram} options between 2 GB to 16 GB. This is comparable to a early- to mid-2010s desktop \glsxtrshort{pc}. It is very compact physically, measuring in at 56mm by 85mm. It contains the necessary connectivity options that make it a feasible option for the \gls{ics}, as it can interface with the automobile via a serial-to-\gls{obdii} adapter, either wired (i.e. \gls{usb}) or wireless (i.e. Bluetooth). Regarding energy consumption, the \gls{rpi} 5 consumes around 12 watts of power over a \gls{usb}-C cable, requiring 5V/5A \gls{dc}.

            \par For the DashAR System, a memory configuration of 8 GB of \gls{ram} and 256 GB of storage via a micro \glsxtrshort{sd} card, was selected as the ideal specification to meet the needs of the application. The Ubuntu Server 24.04 \gls{os} was installed on the device. Additional software packages were installed in order to prepare the device to run the \gls{das} \gls{api} service. Specific details regarding the configuration of the \gls{ics} device can be found in Appendix \ref{appendix:build}.

        \subsubsection{Software}
            \par Once the \gls{ics} device was ready, the implementation process could begin for the \gls{das} \gls{api}. The Python programming language, specifically version 3.12 \cite{PSF2025}, was selected as the language to be used for all server-side programming. While lacking some of the robust and low-level features available in traditional systems languages (e.g. C, C++, Rust, etc.), Python was selected due to its platform independence (i.e. ability to run on Linux, macOS, and Windows), its ease of use when implementing functionality, its turnaround time for prototyping, and its ability to be performant enough to execute the tasks it needed. Given the integrations with \gls{obdii} and the real-time nature of this application, it would have been optimal, from a performance perspective, to select a traditional systems language. However, the learning curve for those languages is much higher, and many functions readily available in Python would have to be manually implemented in those languages.

            \par With the programming language selected, the \gls{das} \gls{api} could be developed. The two most important components that made this \gls{api}, let alone the entire project, possible are the python-obd module and the Tornado web server. \cite{Whitfield2025}'s python-obd module provides the facilities for interfacing with \gls{obdii} in software. This module allows for \gls{obdii} interfacing customization, as it provides parameters for setting the baud rate (ranging from 9,600 to 115,200 \gls{bps}), a specific interface device (e.g. /dev/ttyUSB0 in Linux/macOS, COM1 in Windows), and additional operational configurations (e.g. optimized commands, timeouts, voltage check, low power mode, etc.)

            \par The Tornado web server, developed initially by FriendFeed: a social media feed aggregation company, which was acquired by Meta (formerly Facebook) in 2009, is an asynchronous web framework \cite{TornadoAuthors2025}. There were many candidates for this component of the system, such as the Django and Flask frameworks. However, Tornado stood out for its ability to handle concurrency and latency in a performant way. Additionally, it has proven useful in many real-time applications, such as social applications and gaming.

            \par In addition to these two modules, the following modules are also utilized in various ways:

            \begin{itemize}
                \item os: for interfacing with the \gls{os} functions, like file system manipulation.
                \item json: for parsing and storing configurations.
                \item argpase: for passing command line arguments in on script start. For example, enabling verbosity for debugging purposes.
                \item asyncio: for establishing an event loop and asynchronous functionality, mainly for Tornado's HTTP listeners. This is how Tornado achieves its concurrency.
                \item random: for generating random values, namely when the system is in test mode.
                \item enum: for establishing Enums defining lists of values for a given variable. For example, a SystemStatus enum representing the system's states.
                \item uuid: for generating randomized, UUID-compliant values. These values are assigned as IDs for any and all object within the system.
                \item sqlite3: for interfacing with a SQLite3 database.
                \item importlib: for importing custom extensions for DashAR.
            \end{itemize}

            \par Furthermore, custom modules were developed and stored under a directory called ``das\_core'', which represented the greater module of functionality:

            \begin{itemize}
                \item obdii\_interpreter.py
                \item helper.py
                \item data\_connector.py
                \item configuration.py
            \end{itemize}

            \par The first section of code implemented for the \gls{das} \gls{api} was das\_service.py, found in Appendix \ref{scl:dasservicepy}. This module is the main entry point for the application, and is stored in the root of the \gls{das} directory. The initial version of this file contained very basic GET request handling, used to help learn how the Tornado framework works. Once it was determined how Tornado functions, alongside the asyncio module, basic \gls{obdii} data access and storage was developed, as obdii\_interpreter.py. This file can be found in Appendix \ref{scl:dascoreobdiipy}. When the script started, the \gls{obdii} context would be established. Then, the automobile's \gls{vin} would be retrieved. Within the SQLite3 database on device, a record would be written to DASHAR\_SESSION, indicating a new DashAR session was starting. DASHAR\_SESSION would store the \gls{uuid}, which uniquely represented the session, the \gls{vin} of the automobile, and the current date and time, represented as a UNIX timestamp. Once initialization succeeded, an endpoint was accessible to retrieve the \gls{obdii} data: /data/obdii. When called, this endpoint would retrieve speed, RPM, and fuel level data over the ELM327 interface via the python-obdii module. This data was written to the DASHAR\_SESSION\_DATA table in the SQLite3 database. DASHAR\_SESSION\_DATA contained a key reference back to the DASHAR\_SESSION table, along with another timestamp, as UNIX timestamp, for when the data points were collected, and the data points themselves. Of note, the DashAR System is designed to be client-side event-driven, meaning the data is only logged when a request is made to the system.

            \par After some time, it was found that some code needed to be reused in multiple places throughout the \gls{das} \gls{api}. Therefore, helper.py was created to serve the rest of the \gls{das} \gls{api} as its namesake suggests: a general helper. This module can be found in Appendix \ref{scl:dascorehelperpy}. helper.py contains shared functions (e.g. UUID generation, UNIX timestamp generation, and Python dictionary to JSON marshalling), prepared database statements (e.g. queries, insert statements, etc.), and enums representing the system statuses (i.e. NOT STARTED, STARTING, FAILED, and READY), the service modes (i.e. PRODUCTION, TEST, DEBUG, and INVALID), and other basic lookups (e.g. data formatting, data sources, etc.)

            \par A specific module was developed for handling database transactions: data\_connector.py. This module is responsible for establishing a SQLite3 context, which makes the writing of records to the SQLite3 database possible. Additionally, this module would be responsible for handling file system manipulations (e.g. writing and reading configuration files, writing logs, etc.), However, due to time constraints with the project, this particular functionality was never realized, at least within this module.

            \par Finally, it was realized that customization to the \gls{das} \gls{api}'s data sources and configuration would be necessary. Therefore, the configuration.py module was developed. It can be found in Appendix \ref{scl:dascoreconfigpy}, with an example configuration file in Appendix \ref{scl:configjson}. This module handles the parsing of the configuration file, and tests the configuration for any conflicts. For example, it verifies that the SQLite3 database exists at the specified location on disk. If not, the system will report the error back via the \gls{ics} device's console. Ideally, in the future, this kind of error reporting would be sent to the \gls{hud} application, assuming a connection can be established between the \gls{ics} device and the \gls{ar} subsystem device.

    \subsection{HUD}
        \par After the \gls{das} \gls{api} was in a usable state, the \gls{ar} subsystem was developed.

        \subsubsection{Hardware}
            \par Fortunately, with the hardware aspects of the \gls{ar} subsystem, very little preparation was needed. The XREAL Air 2 Pro \gls{ar} glasses were selected as the target platform for the \gls{hud} software, because of its compactness and ability to superimpose the \gls{hud} into the user's line of sight as they operate the automobile. While it was initially known that there was no compute capability on the \gls{ar} glasses, it was unknown what devices were compatible with the glasses. XREAL provides a listing of supported compute devices, namely their own Beam products: Beam and Beam Pro, and a select offering of smartphones at varying price points. While not on the supported list, an attempt was made to check the Air 2 Pro's compatibility with a readily available Samsung S21 smartphone. Unfortunately, additional software from XREAL was required in order to run \gls{ar} applications. The Samsung S21 could not support that software. It should be noted that the Samsung S21, along with various PCs, Macs, smartphones, gaming consoles, and other electronics with support for \glsxtrshort{hdmi} output are compatible with the Air 2 Pro glasses without additional compute. However, the caveat is the glasses are treated as an external display, and render the output as such.

            \par An advantage that the XREAL Air 2 Pro offers over other solutions is its inclusion of electrochromic dimming: dynamic tinting of the lenses, controlled by the user in set intervals (e.g. 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\%). The purpose of this feature is to enhance immersion when using a \gls{vr}-based application. The lightest tint available is equivalent to a standard pair of sunglasses. While this feature offers a great benefit to drivers using the DashAR System, it also has its drawbacks. The primary drawback: the system can only be used in sunny, or at least well-lit situations. The system cannot be safely used at night, as the lowest lens tint makes it too dark to see. Additionally, it is heavily advised to avoid operating the system in adverse conditions, such as rain and snow. It is the author's hope that one day the system can be adapted to use a different \gls{ar} solution, which would provide an un-tinted lens option.

        \subsubsection{Software}
            \par As a developmental starting point, a mockup of the \gls{hud}'s \gls{ui} was made in the popular, \gls{saas}-based graphic design application Canva. This mockup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:dasharhudmockup}. This mockup demonstrates the utilization of trays and widgets, as described in the design of the \gls{hud}. It is of note, however, that the widgets shown in this mockup are not to suggest that all data points are readily available. Rather, it represents the dynamic nature of the \gls{hud}, and how it can be customized. For example, turn signals and blind spot indicators are shown in the mockup. While some automobiles have this data available over \gls{obdii}, some do not. It is dependent on the manufacturer of the automobile, and the features they provide.

            \begin{figure}[h!]
                \vspace{0.5cm}
                \centering
                \includegraphics[scale=0.225]{./Support/Images/DashAR-UIMockup.png}
                \caption{DashAR HUD UI Mockup}

            \par With the \gls{ui} mockup on hand, the \gls{hud} could be implemented. Out of all of the existing development tools available around \gls{ar} development, the Unity Engine was the ideal choice \cite{UnityTechnologies2025}. At the time of development, Unity Engine 2022.3 was the most recent \gls{lts} version available. Since then, a newer LTS version was released: Unity 6 (or 6000, to keep with the previously used version scheme.) Additionally, XREAL provides an easy-to-use \gls{sdk}: \gls{nrsdk}, which allows Unity projects to be compiled for the XREAL \gls{ar} glasses \cite{XREAL2024}.

            \par Programming in Unity is made possible with the C\# programming language. C\# scripts are attached to objects in a ``scene'', which is the primary metaphor used to express a 3D environment. Scripts attached to objects inheriting MonoBehavior, named ``GameObjects'', which is a base class for Unity which offers various methods for managing execution of code and other functions. Two of the main methods utilized in this application inherited from MonoBehavior are Start and Update. The Start method is invoked when a script initializes at runtime. The engine will execute any code provided within the method before the first frame is rendered. After initialization, the Update method is automatically invoked once per frame, relative to the performance of the engine (e.g. if the application is running at 30 \gls{fps}, the Update method is invoked 30 times per second).

            \par Using this understanding of the MonoBehavior class, the \gls{hud} application performs its operations centered around a state machine. The state machine, defined in Appendix \ref{scl:dasharstatemachinecs}, holds an instance of the \gls{hud} class (Appendix \ref{scl:dasharhudcs}), device contexts, such as the \gls{das} device (Appendix \ref{scl:dashardascs}) and the \gls{ar} subsystem device (Appendix \ref{scl:dashardevicecs}), as well as configuration data. There are also special classes for handling \gls{http} responses, which are demarshalled \gls{json}, defined at the request level.

            \par In regard to the DashAR \gls{hud} application, all of the persistent objects needed for the entire application's run time are created during the Start method's invocation. This is explicitly the state machine, which in turn instantiates the other classes. After initialization, the Update method is invoked. A check is performed to determine if the application should poll the \gls{das} device for a data update. The check succeeds every five frames. If the check does not succeed (i.e. between the fifth frames), no action is taken. It should be noted that the ``every fifth frame'' check is completely arbitrary. However, the ideal of including this type of check is to ensure that the system is able to respond in a timely fashion, and reduces the chances of a potential request overload on the \gls{das} device.

        \subsubsection{HUD Customizations}
            \par To achieve a truly customizable \gls{hud}, two types of configuration files are used: Base configuration and Widget configuration. The file format used for these configuration files is \gls{json}. \gls{json} was selected for its versatility, its platform agnosticism, and its ease of use. Both Python and C\# support \gls{json} parsing and writing. Another potential consideration for a performant serialization format for data transmission, especially for this type of application, would be Google's \gls{protobufs}. \gls{protobufs} are known for their compactness and optimizations \cite{GoogleLLC2025}. However, more work is involved in dealing with \gls{protobufs}, such as preparing the proto files via protoc, the \gls{protobufs} compiler. With \gls{json}, data is readily available out of the box.

            \par base.json, as shown in Appendix \ref{appendix:hudbaseconfigjson}, defines the general positions of the trays and tray anchors. Additionally, a palette of base widgets are defined. These base widgets, Circles (Small and Large) and Rectangles (Large), provide a means of display critical and non-critical information while operating the automobile. The actual widget definitions refer to these base widgets in order to know how they need to be formatted.

            \par default.json, as shown in Appendix \ref{appendix:huddefaultconfigjson}, define the widgets to be used, their target tray anchor, and their data source. For example, a Speedometer widget would have a defined data point (i.e. speed), a data source (i.e. \gls{obdii}), and a tray anchor (i.e. B3). Given the constraints set in the design specification, only 10 active entries can exist in the configuration.

            \par While not implemented during this research due to time constraints, the customizations made to the \gls{hud} interface would be reflected in JSON files that look very similar to default.json. However, the main difference is the new file would be referenced in the base.json file, instead of default.json. A web-based application, ideally, would exist, hosted by the \gls{ics}, to allow for direct customization of the \gls{hud}.

First, the lighting of the environment causes the dynamically generated version to appear brighter. In turn, it is more difficult to discern the boundaries of the trays and widgets' edges.
